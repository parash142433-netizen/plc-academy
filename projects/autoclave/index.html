Building a steam sterilizer PLC program from scratch

A step-by-step reconstruction of how an automation engineer would design and write the control program for a pre-vacuum steam sterilizer (autoclave). It is an anonymised example based on a real program listing (.awl) and its project file (.mwp). Each step explains the problem, the thinking, the real code and how to make it robust. It ends with a live simulator of the finished program.

Build steps
    Understand

    Start with the process, not the PLC

    A steam sterilizer has one job: expose every surface of the load to saturated steam at a known temperature for a known time, and prove it. Everything in the program serves that job. Air must be removed first, because air pockets stay colder than steam at the same pressure. The load must be dried or cooled before it is handled, and it may only leave through the sterile-side door.

    Engineer's thinking

    What must happen, and in what order?
    Lock the doors, remove the air with vacuum pulses, heat to the sterilisation temperature, hold it for the exposure time, then dry under vacuum (or cool, for liquids), vent to atmosphere and release the load.
    What decides success?
    Temperature at or above setpoint for the whole exposure, or an equivalent lethality (F0). The program must measure both and record that they were reached.
    What can hurt people?
    A door opened under pressure or vacuum, or onto 120 °C steam. Door interlocks are the most important logic in the machine.
    What protects the product?
    A pass-through rule: load from the loading side, unload only from the sterile side, and never have both doors open.
    Who else needs data?
    An operator panel for recipes and alarms, and a supervisory system (two Ethernet modules) for cycle records.

    Understand

    List the I/O and choose the hardware

    An autoclave has many more valves than sensors. Count the signals first: they decide the CPU, the expansion modules and how the program is layered.

    The count

    • 35 digital inputs: door positions and locks, pushbuttons, service pressure switches, valve position feedbacks, safety switches
    • 47 digital outputs: valves, door drives and seals, vacuum pump, lamps, beacon and horn
    • 6 analog inputs: temperatures and pressures
    • 1 analog output: the proportional steam valve

    The hardware it implies

    • CPU 226: 24 DI / 16 DO on board, so the rest sits on expansion modules (addresses I2–I4, I10–I11, Q2–Q5, Q10)
    • Analog module(s) for AIW0–AIW10 and AQW0
    • Two CP 243-1 Ethernet modules (wizard blocks for positions 0 and 3)
    • An operator panel writing recipes and commands into V memory

    Watch the expansion limit

    A CPU 226 accepts at most seven expansion modules. Digital expansion for 11 more inputs and 31 more outputs, analog modules and two Ethernet modules use most of that budget. Count modules, not just points, before promising a customer "one more valve".

    Two Ethernet modules, one in use

    Two traps with wizard blocks

    • ETH0_CTRL exists but is never called. If a module really sits at position 0, it is configured but never serviced; if not, the block is clutter that confuses the next engineer.
    • Aux is called twice per scan (step 4), so ETH3_CTRL runs twice too. Check that the wizard block tolerates that, or move the call out of Aux.

    A wiring layer: the logic never touches I or Q

    Two blocks stand between the field and the logic. Ent_Cliente (customer inputs) copies every physical input into V memory, inverting the normally-closed ones. Sal_Cliente (customer outputs) is the only block that writes Q, from demand bits V10.0–V17.7. All other logic reads and writes V bits only.

    Why the layer pays

    • A machine wired differently, or a moved input, changes two blocks and nothing else.
    • Every signal can be simulated or forced in V memory without touching the physical image.
    • Fail-safe inversion happens once, in one place, so the logic always reads "1 = healthy".

    Plan

    Plan the memory map and the units

    The panel, the supervisory system and the logic all share V memory. Decide the blocks first, and decide the units: this program works entirely in integers, so every value has an implied scale.

    V memory map of the sterilizerClick a block to see what lives there

      Engineer's thinking

      Integers with a fixed scale
      Temperatures are tenths of a degree (1210 = 121.0 °C), times are tenths of a minute (200 = 20.0 min), pressures are whole mbar absolute. INT maths is fast and exact on an S7-200, and the panel shows the decimal point.
      One byte per alarm
      VB600–VB689 give every alarm its own byte: the live condition, its history, horn and acknowledgement bits. One generic subroutine handles them all (step 14).
      An auto/manual pair for every output
      VB20–VB27 hold an "auto" bit for each demand bit and VB30–VB37 a "manual on" bit. The data block stores VB20–VB27 = 255: everything in auto.
      Options as bits
      VB800–VB808 switch features on or off for this machine variant (second door, second probe, liquids counter-pressure).

      Options nobody can switch on

      Several option bits are read but never written by the program or stored in the data block, such as V801.3, V803.0, V804.0 and V806.4. If the panel doesn't write them either, their branches are dead code. Step 15 shows one that would break the machine if someone did switch it on.

      Design the program structure

      OB1 is ten networks long. It reads like a table of contents: wiring in, protections, doors and start conditions, the three cycles, the actuator logic, and wiring out. Almost everything waits for a 10-second power-up timer.

      BlockThe question it answersWhy it is a separate block
      Ent_Cliente / Sal_ClienteWhich wire is which?Keeps wiring changes out of the logic
      Ent_Analog + Aux_EscaladoWhat are the measurements in units?One parameterised routine for every channel, ranges in data
      Alarmas + Aux_AlmIs anything wrong, and what does it stop?One byte and one handler per alarm; one abort group

      Passwords in a PLC

      Passwords here are integers (at most 32767, digits only) kept in V memory, which any panel or supervisory connection can read. An S7-200 can't protect them, and it can't keep an audit trail of who did what. Keep user management on the panel or supervisory system, which can. Remove unused library blocks, so nobody assumes the PLC is enforcing access.

      A call that looks like a mistake

      OB1 calls Aux twice per scan: in network 1 and again at the end of network 5. It looks like a copy-paste error, but it isn't. When a cycle's last step clears the step bits, the second call updates the "cycle running" flags in the same scan. Without it, the chamber block runs one scan with stale flags. In the simulator that briefly starts the vacuum pump after every cycle. The side effect is that ETH3_CTRL and the clock read inside Aux also run twice. A one-line comment would have saved every reader the doubt.

      Robustness habits

      • Give I/O, drives and panels time to boot before trusting them. Gate the protections and the outputs, not just the alarms.
      • Count phase times on a pulse that stops during an abort, so a held cycle doesn't keep "earning" time.
      • Keep machine options in one block of bits, set in one place.

      Two traps in five lines

      • T32 is a 1 ms timer. These update asynchronously, so its bit can change between two OB1 networks in the same scan: one block runs, the next doesn't. A 100 ms timer (T37 and up, preset 100) gives the same 10 s without that.
      • Setting options on the first scan overrides anything the panel stored. That's fine for fixed hardware options. For anything an operator may change, keep the value in the data block instead.

      Robustness habits

      • Write the general linear map once (input range to output range), with a guard for a zero span.
      • Clamp the result. A slightly under-range transmitter shouldn't produce a negative temperature that some comparison later misreads.
      • Detect both a broken loop (under-range) and a shorted or saturated one (over-range).

      Where to put the over-range limit

      The check treats raw ≥ 32000 as a failure. That is exactly 20.0 mA, a legitimate full-scale reading. With ranges of 150 °C and 4000 mbar the process never gets there, so it is harmless here. In general, put the over-range limit above full scale (NAMUR NE 43 uses about 21 mA, raw 33600).

      Robustness habits

      • Validate in the PLC, every scan, even when the panel validates too. Panels get replaced and supervisory systems write directly.
      • Derive dependent limits from the value they depend on, so the order "sterilise < control < over-temperature" can never be broken.
      • Clamp rather than reject when the safe value is obvious, and show the operator the value actually in use.

      The one entry that isn't checked

      The number of drying pulses VW154 (stored value 1) is the only recipe value with no clamp. A typing slip of 10 makes drying ten times longer, and a negative value ends drying after one pulse.

      Engineer's thinking: why step bits and not a step number?

      Step bits make every transition a self-contained network: R the old step, S the new one. Branches (more pulses, or drying, or cooling) are just two S instructions under different conditions. The price is that nothing stops two steps being set at once except discipline. That's why GP_Fases exists, and why a step number is easier to log and to check.

      Build

      Air removal: vacuum pulses

      Steam only reaches its saturation temperature where there is no air. One deep vacuum isn't enough for porous loads, so the program alternates vacuum and steam: each pulse dilutes the remaining air.

      Engineer's thinking: why air is the enemy

      Pressure in the chamber is the sum of the steam and air partial pressures (Dalton's law). The thermometer sees the saturation temperature of the steam part only. At 2057 mbar with 10 % air left, the steam is at 1851 mbar, which saturates at about 118 °C instead of 121 °C. Pressure says "sterilising", but the load isn't. Three pulses from 900 to 100 mbar cut the air to roughly (100/900)³, about 0.1 % of what was there.

      The first vacuum that never happens

      Network 5 leaves the first-vacuum step M10.2 when the pressure is reached or when VW204 ≥ VW110. But VW204 only counts in the next step, and the stored hold time VW110 is 0. So 0 ≥ 0 at once: the first vacuum and its hold pass in one scan, whatever the pressure. The first pulse's vacuum does the job instead, so the cycle still works, but the "first vacuum level" VW108 has no effect. Watch the event log in the simulator.

      Robustness habits

      • Count repeats with a counter that is cleared at cycle start (here by Aux_Borra, which zeroes VW200–VW399), never by a retentive value left from the last cycle.
      • Confirm every pressure target for a second before acting, so a pressure spike doesn't end a step.
      • Give each exit condition its own counter. A shared one couples steps in ways nobody intended.

      Two clocks

      Exposure and F0 count on M0.0, the raw one-second pulse. Every other phase counts on M0.5, which stops during an abort (step 5). If a cycle is held at temperature after an alarm, exposure and F0 keep growing. Whether held time should count is a validation decision. Make it deliberately and write it down.

      Robustness habits

      • Count exposure only while the condition holds, and say in the specification whether a dip pauses or restarts it (here it pauses).
      • Integer accumulators with a carry keep long counts exact. The remainder in VW238 is never lost.
      • Use a time base that can't overflow: one carry per call is enough as long as one second never adds more than the base (true up to about 138 °C here).

      An abort with no way out

      An abort sets M9.0. Aux clears it only when no cycle is running, but the only way the steam cycle ends is network 25's 30 s timer, and that timer has AN M9.0 in it. So after an abort the program can never return to idle by itself: something outside it, most likely the panel, must clear the step bits or M9.0. If the panel does, document it in the specification. If not, it is a lock-up. The simulator's "Cancel cycle" button plays that panel's part.

      Prove

      Test, commission and review

      A sterilizer is proven with instruments that don't belong to the PLC. Here is the order a careful engineer follows, and what a fresh review of this program found.

      1. Desk check. Read every network against the requirements in step 1. Compile, and clear every warning.
      2. Simulate. Force inputs on the bench and walk every step, every transition and every interlock with the status chart.
      3. I/O and loop check. Prove each wire and valve feedback. Check each analog at 4, 12 and 20 mA against a calibrator, including the over- and under-range alarms.
      4. Door challenge. Try to open each door under pressure, under vacuum, above 80 °C and with the other door open. Every attempt must be refused.
      5. Leak and air-removal tests. Run the vacuum leak test (step 16's "Start leak test") and an air-removal test pack. Both must pass before any load cycle.
      6. Temperature studies. Empty-chamber distribution and loaded heat penetration with independent thermocouples. Compare against the PLC's temperature, exposure time and F0 for the same cycle.
      7. Alarm challenge. Trigger every abort and forced-finish condition on purpose, and check what the cycle and the load-sterile flag do.
      8. Archive. Upload the final program and data block, record the versions, and read the recipe back from the running PLC: the stored values on this page are download values, not necessarily what runs.

      What a fresh review found

      None of these stop the machine running normal cycles, which is how they survived. Several only matter if an option is switched on or a fault happens.

      What this program does well

      • A wiring layer on both sides, so the logic never touches I or Q.
      • One general scaling routine with a zero-span guard, and every result clamped.
      • Every recipe entry validated in the PLC, with limits that keep dependent values in order.
      • A "reasons not to start" word, and a pass-through door barrier in the same place as the safety interlocks.
      • One byte and one handler per alarm, with separate abort and forced-finish reactions.
      • Pressure control on the saturation curve with a temperature trim, clamped between table pressures.

            Things to try

            • Press Start cycle at 60×. Watch three vacuum pulses, heat-up, 20 min exposure and drying in the trend, about 38 min of machine time.
            • Watch the log at the start: the first vacuum steps pass in the same instant (finding F4).
            • Tick Steam valve stuck during exposure: over-temperature forces a finish, and the load is never flagged sterile.
            • Tick Chamber probe wire break mid-cycle: the abort freezes everything until you press Cancel cycle (finding F1).
            • After a good cycle, door 1 stays disabled (M1.6 off): only the sterile side can open. Open door 2, close it and press Start: refused, because the next load must go in through door 1.
            • Run the Leak test with and without the leak fault.

            What is real and what is modelled

            • Real: the logic of the steam cycle, leak test, start conditions, door permissives, chamber and jacket valves, steam table, F0, recipe limits and the alarm reactions, with the stored values.
            • Modelled: steam, air and heat in the chamber and jacket, the load, valve travel and every field device.
            • Simplified: doors are closed/open inputs (their motors and seals are not simulated), the filter cycle and manual mode are left out, and the panel's advance, abort and cancel are assumed.
