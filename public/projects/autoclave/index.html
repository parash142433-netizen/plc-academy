Basic Autoclave project · Siemens S7-200 · CPU 226

Build a steam sterilizer PLC program, step by step

In this tutorial you build the control program for a pre-vacuum steam sterilizer (autoclave) on a Siemens S7-200. Each step states the problem, the design thinking, the code for that step and the habits that make it robust. The last steps test the program, and a simulator runs everything you have built.

Build steps
    Understand

    Start with the process, not the PLC

    A steam sterilizer has one job: expose every surface of the load to saturated steam at a known temperature for a known time, and prove it. Everything in the program serves that job. Air must be removed first, because air pockets stay colder than steam at the same pressure. The load must be dried or cooled before it is handled, and it may only leave through the sterile-side door.

    Engineer's thinking

    What must happen, and in what order?
    Lock the doors, remove the air with a first vacuum and vacuum pulses, heat to the sterilisation temperature, hold it for the exposure time, then dry under vacuum (or cool, for liquids), vent to atmosphere and release the load.
    What decides success?
    Temperature at or above setpoint for the whole exposure, and the lethality (F0) the load received. The program measures both and flags the load sterile only when both targets are met.
    What can hurt people?
    A door opened under pressure or vacuum, or onto 120 °C steam. The door interlocks are the most important logic in the machine.
    What protects the product?
    A pass-through rule: load from the loading side, unload only from the sterile side, and never have both doors open.
    Who else needs data?
    An operator panel for recipes and alarms, and a supervisory system on Ethernet for cycle records.

    Understand

    List the I/O and choose the hardware

    An autoclave has many more valves than sensors. Count the signals first: they decide the CPU, the expansion modules and how you layer the program.

    The count

    • 35 digital inputs: door positions and locks, pushbuttons, service pressure switches, valve position feedbacks, safety switches
    • 47 digital outputs: valves, door drives and seals, vacuum pump, lamps, beacon and horn
    • 6 analog inputs: temperatures and pressures
    • 1 analog output: the proportional steam valve

    The hardware it needs

    • CPU 226: 24 DI / 16 DO on board; the rest on expansion modules (addresses I2–I4, I10–I11, Q2–Q5, Q10)
    • Analog modules for AIW0–AIW10 and AQW0
    • CP 243-1 Ethernet module at module position 3
    • An operator panel writing recipes and commands into V and M memory

    Watch the expansion limit

    A CPU 226 accepts at most seven expansion modules. Digital expansion for 11 more inputs and 31 more outputs, the analog modules and the Ethernet module use most of that budget. Count modules, not just points, before promising "one more valve".

    Why the layer pays

    • A machine wired differently, or a moved input, changes two blocks and nothing else.
    • Every signal can be simulated or forced in V memory without touching the physical image.
    • Fail-safe inversion happens once, in one place, so the logic always reads "1 = healthy".

    Plan

    Plan the memory map and the units

    The panel, the supervisory system and the logic all share V memory. Decide the blocks first, and decide the units: this program works entirely in integers, so every value has an implied scale.

    V memory map of the sterilizerClick a block to see what lives there

      Engineer's thinking

      Integers with a fixed scale
      Temperatures are tenths of a degree (1210 = 121.0 °C), times are tenths of a minute (200 = 20.0 min), pressures are whole mbar absolute. INT maths is fast and exact on an S7-200, and the panel shows the decimal point.
      One byte per alarm
      VB600–VB689 give every alarm its own byte: the live condition, its history, horn and acknowledgement bits. One generic subroutine handles them all (step 14).
      An auto/manual pair for every output
      VB20–VB27 hold an "auto" bit for each demand bit and VB30–VB37 a "manual on" bit. The data block sets VB20–VB27 to 255: everything starts in auto.
      Options as bits
      VB800–VB808 switch features on or off for a machine variant: second door, second probe, liquids counter-pressure.

      Options that stay off

      Some option bits are read by the logic but left off on this machine: V801.3 (wait for services before start), V803.0, V804.0 and the spare probes V806.2–V806.5. Their branches stay inactive. Before you switch one on, read every network it enables and test it in the simulator or on the bench. An option branch that nobody has run is untested code.

      Design the program structure

      Engineer's thinking: why call Aux twice?

      When a cycle's last step clears the step bits, the "cycle running" flags computed at the top of the scan are already out of date. Calling Aux again right after the cycle blocks recomputes them, so the chamber block never runs a scan with stale flags. That is also why Aux holds nothing else: anything that must run exactly once per scan, such as the Ethernet block, lives in Comms. Leave a comment on the second call so the next reader doesn't remove it as a duplicate.

      Optional: operator logins in the PLC

      The panel handles logins in this design. If a panel can't, the PLC can keep tables of user numbers, passwords, access levels and failed attempts, addressed through pointers: Aux_GP_PassEsc writes an entry, Aux_GP_PassLee reads one, and Aux_GP_PassErr validates a request. They hold no users or passwords of their own. Call them from the routine that serves the panel's login screen; the program in this tutorial doesn't need them.

      Passwords in a PLC

      Passwords here are integers (at most 32767, digits only) kept in V memory, which any panel or supervisory connection can read. An S7-200 can't protect them, and it can't keep an audit trail of who did what. Prefer the panel's or supervisory system's user management, which can. If you don't use these helpers, leave them out of the project so nobody assumes the PLC is enforcing access.

      Robustness habits

      • Give I/O, drives and panels time to boot before trusting them. Gate the protections and the outputs, not just the alarms.
      • Count phase times on a pulse that stops during an abort, so a held cycle doesn't keep earning time.
      • Keep machine options in one block of bits, set in one place.

      Why a 100 ms timer for the gate

      • A 1 ms timer (T32, T96) updates asynchronously, every millisecond, so its bit can change between two OB1 networks in the same scan: one block runs, the next doesn't. A 100 ms timer updates once per scan, when its instruction executes.
      • Setting options on the first scan overrides anything the panel wrote. That's right for fixed hardware options. For anything an operator may change, keep the value in the data block instead.

      Robustness habits

      • Write the general linear map once (input range to output range), with a guard for a zero span.
      • Clamp the result, so a slightly under-range transmitter can't produce a negative temperature that a later comparison misreads.
      • Detect both a broken loop (under-range) and a shorted or saturated one (over-range). Put the over-range limit above full scale: a reading of exactly 20 mA is a valid 100 %.

      Robustness habits

      • Validate in the PLC, every scan, even when the panel validates too. Panels get replaced and supervisory systems write directly.
      • Derive dependent limits from the value they depend on, so the order "sterilise < control < over-temperature" can never be broken.
      • Clamp every entry, including counts. A typing slip of 10 drying pulses would make drying ten times longer.
      • Clamp rather than reject when the safe value is obvious, and show the operator the value actually in use.

      Engineer's thinking: why step bits and not a step number?

      Step bits make every transition a self-contained network: R the old step, S the new one. Branches (more pulses, or drying, or cooling) are just two S instructions under different conditions. The price is that nothing stops two steps being set at once except discipline. That is one reason GP_Fases exists, and why a step number is easier to log and to check.

      Air removal: first vacuum and vacuum pulses

      Steam only reaches its saturation temperature where there is no air. One deep vacuum isn't enough for porous loads, so after the first vacuum the program alternates steam and vacuum: each pulse dilutes the air that is left.

      Engineer's thinking: why air is the enemy

      Pressure in the chamber is the sum of the steam and air partial pressures (Dalton's law). The thermometer sees the saturation temperature of the steam part only. At 2057 mbar with 10 % air left, the steam is at 1851 mbar, which saturates at about 118 °C instead of 121 °C. Pressure says "sterilising", but the load isn't. Each pulse from 900 to 100 mbar keeps about 100/900 of the air, so three pulses leave roughly (100/900)³, about 0.1 % of what was there.

      Common mistake: one counter, two steps

      The hold counter VW204 runs only in the hold step M10.3. If you also used "VW204 ≥ VW110" as an exit of the first-vacuum step M10.2, a hold time of 0 would make 0 ≥ 0 true at once, and the first vacuum would be skipped whatever the pressure. Give each step's exit its own condition. In the simulator's event log, the first vacuum runs for about 35 s before the first steam pulse.

      Robustness habits

      • Count repeats with a counter that is cleared at every cycle start (here Aux_Borra zeroes VW200–VW399), never one left over from the last cycle.
      • Confirm every pressure target for a second before acting, so a pressure spike can't end a step.

      Engineer's thinking: which clock?

      Exposure and F0 count on M0.0, the plain one-second pulse, not on the abort-gated M0.5. F0 is the lethality the load physically received, so it should keep counting. And an abort before the exposure is complete can't produce a sterile load: the aborted cycle can only end by being cancelled (step 13), and the sterile flag is set only while the cycle is still running with both targets reached. Write that decision into your specification; validators will ask.

      Robustness habits

      • Count exposure only while the condition holds, and say in the specification whether a dip pauses or restarts it (here it pauses).
      • Integer accumulators with a carry keep long counts exact; the remainder in VW238 is never lost.
      • Use a time base that can't overflow. One carry per call is enough as long as one second never adds more than the base, which holds up to about 138 °C here.

      Getting out of an abort

      An abort (M9.0, step 14) freezes the cycle where it is: phase timers stop and the valves close. That is the safe reaction, but the cycle can't finish by itself, because the final step's 30 s timer is also stopped by M9.0. So give the operator a way out. The panel's "cancel cycle" button sets M8.4, and Aux network 1 (step 5) clears every step byte. With no cycle running, Aux clears M9.0, and the idle chamber logic takes over: it exhausts pressure above atmospheric and lets sterile air in below it. The doors unlock once the chamber is near atmospheric and below 80 °C.

      Robustness habits

      • Give every abort a defined way back to idle, inside the program. A machine shouldn't depend on an engineer with a laptop to recover.
      • Make idle a safe state in its own right: here, idle with the doors closed always brings the chamber back to atmospheric pressure.
      • Record why a cycle ended. An abort after the exposure has finished leaves a sterile but possibly wet load; the panel should say the drying was not completed.

      Prove

      Test and commission

      A sterilizer is proven with instruments that don't belong to the PLC. Work through the program in this order, then check it against the list of common mistakes below.

      1. Desk check. Read every network against the requirements in step 1. Compile, and clear every warning.
      2. Simulate. Force inputs on the bench and walk every step, every transition and every interlock with the status chart.
      3. I/O and loop check. Prove each wire and valve feedback. Check each analog input at 4, 12 and 20 mA against a calibrator, including the under- and over-range alarms.
      4. Door challenge. Try to open each door under pressure, under vacuum, above 80 °C and with the other door open. Every attempt must be refused.
      5. Leak and air-removal tests. Run the vacuum leak test (the "Leak test" button in step 16) and an air-removal test pack. Both must pass before any load cycle.
      6. Temperature studies. Empty-chamber distribution and loaded heat penetration with independent thermocouples. Compare them with the PLC's temperature, exposure time and F0 for the same cycle.
      7. Alarm challenge. Trigger every abort and forced-finish condition on purpose, check what the cycle and the load-sterile flag do, and cancel each aborted cycle back to idle.
      8. Archive. Upload the final program and data block, record the versions, and read the recipe back from the running PLC so the record shows what actually runs.

      Common mistakes to check for

      These slip into batch-machine programs easily and survive normal cycles, because they only show up after a fault, an option change or an unusual entry. This tutorial's code avoids each of them; check your own the same way.

      Patterns this tutorial uses

      • A wiring layer on both sides, so the logic never touches I or Q.
      • One general scaling routine with a zero-span guard, and every result clamped.
      • Every recipe entry validated in the PLC, with limits that keep dependent values in order.
      • A "reasons not to start" word, and a pass-through door barrier in the same place as the safety interlocks.
      • One byte and one handler per alarm, with separate abort and forced-finish reactions, and a cancel path back to idle.
      • Pressure control on the saturation curve with a temperature trim, clamped between table pressures.

            Things to try

            • Press Start cycle at 60×. Watch the first vacuum, three vacuum pulses, heat-up, 20 min of exposure and drying in the trend: about 40 min of machine time.
            • Tick Steam valve stuck during exposure: over-temperature forces a finish, and the load is never flagged sterile.
            • Tick Chamber probe wire break mid-cycle: the abort freezes the cycle. Press Cancel cycle and watch the idle logic bring the chamber back to atmospheric pressure.
            • Untick V7.4 clean steam during heat-up: after 45 s the steam valve closes, after 60 s the alarm sounds, and the valve stays closed until you tick it again.
            • After a good cycle, door 1 stays disabled (M1.6 off): only the sterile side can open. Open door 2, close it and press Start cycle: refused, because the next load must go in through door 1.
            • Run the Leak test with and without the leak fault.

            What the simulator runs and what it models

            • Runs: the logic from steps 5–14 — the steam cycle, leak test, start conditions, door permissives, chamber and jacket valves, steam table, F0, recipe limits and the alarm reactions — with the data-block values.
            • Models: steam, air and heat in the chamber and jacket, the load, valve travel and every field device.
            • Left out: door motors and seals (doors are closed/open inputs), the filter cycle and manual mode.
